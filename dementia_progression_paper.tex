% ABSTRACT
% =================================================================================
\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Background and Objective:} Early identification of patients at risk of progressing from Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) to Alzheimer's Disease (AD) is critical for therapeutic intervention. However, current state-of-the-art predictive models predominantly rely on expensive neuroimaging (MRI/PET) or invasive cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers, limiting their accessibility in primary care and resource-limited settings. This study aims to develop a high-performance, cost-effective, and ethically robust machine learning framework to forecast dementia progression using \textit{exclusively} routinely collected clinical data.

\vspace{0.5em}
\textbf{Methods:} We utilized longitudinal data from the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC) Uniform Data Set, comprising 150,904 clinical visits from 37,633 unique patients across 40+ Alzheimer's Disease Research Centers. We formulated the prediction task as a ``Next-Visit Forecasting'' problem, predicting the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) at time $t+1$ based on data from time $t$. We developed a robust gradient boosting framework significantly optimized for clinical tabular data, employing BorderlineSMOTE to address severe class imbalance (4.7:1 ratio). We implemented a rigorous patient-level GroupShuffleSplit validation strategy to prevent data leakage and conducted extensive auditing for demographic fairness and error severity. A complex ensemble approach was also evaluated for comparison.

\vspace{0.5em}
\textbf{Results:} On an independent test set of 29,939 patient visits from 7,527 patients, the proposed framework achieved an overall accuracy of \textbf{88.61\%} and a macro F1-score of \textbf{85.63\%}. Class-specific performance showed: Normal (F1=0.94), MCI (F1=0.83), Mild Dementia (F1=0.76), and Severe Dementia (F1=0.89). Error severity analysis revealed that 95\% of misclassifications were ``1-stage'' errors (e.g., predicting MCI instead of Mild), with catastrophic errors (3-stages off) comprising only 0.1\%. Fairness analysis demonstrated consistent performance across sex groups and racial categories. SHAP-based interpretability analysis identified diagnosis trajectory features, age-diagnosis interactions, and cardiovascular comorbidities as the strongest predictors. Notably, our optimized single-model approach outperformed a complex ensemble, validating the principle of parsimony in clinical AI.

\vspace{0.5em}
\textbf{Conclusions:} This study demonstrates that robust dementia risk stratification is achievable without expensive neuroimaging, using only routine clinical assessments. The proposed framework offers a scalable, transparent, and demographically fair tool for clinical decision support, particularly suitable for deployment in community healthcare and resource-constrained environments. The clinical-only approach addresses the accessibility gap in current AI models, supporting a ``Frugal AI'' paradigm that democratizes early Alzheimer's detection.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder and the leading cause of dementia, affecting over 55 million people worldwide~\cite{alz2024}. As the global population ages, the prevalence of AD is projected to triple by 2050, imposing an unsustainable burden on healthcare systems and economies. The transitional phase between normal cognitive aging and dementia, known as Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI), represents a critical window for therapeutic intervention. Consequently, identifying which MCI patients will progress to dementia---and predicting their future cognitive state---has become a paramount objective in computational neurology and precision medicine.

\subsection{The Accessibility Gap in Current AI Models}

Recent advances in Artificial Intelligence (AI) and machine learning have yielded models capable of predicting AD progression with remarkable accuracy~\cite{doe2023}. However, the vast majority of these studies rely on multimodal data, incorporating Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), Positron Emission Tomography (PET), or cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers (e.g., Amyloid-$\beta$, tau proteins). While scientifically powerful, these modalities present significant barriers to widespread clinical adoption:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Cost and Invasiveness:} PET scans cost \$3,000--\$6,000 per examination, and lumbar punctures for CSF analysis are invasive procedures requiring specialist facilities, making them unsuitable for routine screening in primary care settings.
    \item \textbf{Geographic Availability:} Advanced neuroimaging facilities are often concentrated in specialized academic medical centers, leaving rural communities and low-income populations systematically underserved.
    \item \textbf{Data Scarcity and Overfitting:} Models trained on dense imaging datasets are frequently prone to overfitting due to smaller sample sizes compared to electronic health records (EHR), limiting their generalizability.
    \item \textbf{Scalability Constraints:} Imaging-based approaches cannot scale to population-level screening programs required for early intervention strategies.
\end{enumerate}

There is therefore an urgent, unmet clinical need for ``Frugal AI'' solutions---models that achieve diagnostic excellence using only non-invasive, cost-effective clinical variables (e.g., demographics, cognitive assessments, medical history, functional status) that can be routinely collected during standard clinical encounters.

\subsection{Methodological Challenges: Imbalance and Fairness}

Beyond data modality considerations, two methodological challenges frequently undermine the clinical validity of machine learning models in dementia research. First, medical datasets exhibit inherent class imbalance; patients with severe dementia are substantially rarer than healthy controls, leading standard algorithms to optimize for majority classes while neglecting the most clinically critical cases. Our dataset demonstrates a 4.7:1 imbalance ratio between the most and least prevalent classes.

Second, algorithmic fairness has emerged as a critical concern in healthcare AI~\cite{obermeyer2019}. Models trained on demographically skewed populations (predominantly white, highly educated, urban-dwelling participants) may fail to generalize equitably to diverse demographic groups, potentially exacerbating existing healthcare disparities. This concern is particularly acute in dementia research, where socioeconomic factors influence both disease presentation and healthcare access.

\subsection{Contributions and Novelty}

To address these gaps, this study presents a comprehensive framework for robust prediction of dementia progression using \textit{exclusively} NACC clinical data. Our key contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical-Only Next-Visit Forecasting:} We demonstrate state-of-the-art performance using an optimized Gradient Boosting Machine (XGBoost), proving that expensive neuroimaging is not strictly necessary for accurate risk stratification. Unlike static cross-sectional classification, our model explicitly predicts the \textit{future} diagnostic state ($t+1$), providing actionable prognostic information. We explicitly show that a single, well-tuned model outperforms complex ensembles, adhering to the principle of "Frugal AI".
    
    \item \textbf{Rigorous Validation with Leakage Prevention:} We implement patient-level GroupShuffleSplit validation, ensuring no patient's data appears in both training and test sets---a common methodological flaw in longitudinal studies that artificially inflates performance metrics.
    
    \item \textbf{Comprehensive Fairness Audit:} We conduct systematic performance evaluation across Age, Sex, and Race subgroups, quantifying disparities and ensuring the model is equitable for diverse populations.
    
    \item \textbf{Clinically-Meaningful Error Analysis:} Beyond aggregate accuracy, we analyze the \textit{severity} of misclassifications (1-stage vs. 2-stage vs. 3-stage errors), demonstrating that even when the model errs, predictions remain clinically proximal to ground truth.
    
    \item \textbf{Interpretable Feature Attribution:} We employ SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values to provide transparent, clinician-interpretable explanations of model predictions, aligning with regulatory requirements for explainable AI in healthcare.
\end{itemize}

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Dataset and Study Population}

Data were obtained from the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC) Uniform Data Set (UDS)~\cite{nacc2022}. The NACC database aggregates longitudinal clinical data from over 40 Alzheimer's Disease Research Centers (ADRCs) across the United States, representing one of the largest and most comprehensive dementia research cohorts globally.

\textbf{Inclusion Criteria:} We selected participants who had at least two consecutive clinical visits to facilitate longitudinal prediction (enabling $t \rightarrow t+1$ forecasting). \textbf{Exclusion Criteria:} Visits with missing target labels (Clinical Dementia Rating) were excluded.

The final dataset comprised \textbf{150,904 clinical visits} from \textbf{37,633 unique patients}, with an average of 4.0 visits per patient (range: 1--19 visits). Table~\ref{tab:demographics} presents the demographic characteristics of the study population stratified by current diagnostic status.

\subsection{Feature Engineering}

We extracted and engineered 101 clinical features categorized into six domains:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Demographics (7 features):} Age, age$^2$, age-risk buckets (clinical thresholds at 65, 75, 85 years), years in study, baseline age.
    
    \item \textbf{Diagnosis Trajectory (7 features):} Current diagnostic state (one-hot encoded), historical diagnoses (lag-1, lag-2, lag-3), progression indicators, stability scores, diagnosis mean and variance across patient history, worst diagnosis ever recorded. All historical diagnosis features---including \texttt{diagnosis\_mean}, \texttt{worst\_diagnosis\_ever}, and all lag features (lag-1, lag-2, lag-3)---were computed strictly from visits prior to the current visit $t$, with no look-ahead into future observations. This design eliminates any temporal data leakage concern in the feature construction pipeline.
    
    \item \textbf{Genetic Risk (5 features):} APOE $\epsilon$4 allele status (carrier vs. non-carrier, homozygous vs. heterozygous), APOE4-age interaction terms.
    
    \item \textbf{Comorbidity Burden (6 features):} Cardiovascular conditions (hypertension, atrial fibrillation, congestive heart failure, stroke, TIA), neurological conditions (seizures, TBI, Parkinson's), metabolic conditions (diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, thyroid disorders, B12 deficiency), composite comorbidity scores.
    
    \item \textbf{Temporal Change Features (10 features):} Visit-to-visit changes in BMI, blood pressure (systolic/diastolic), heart rate, weight; rolling means for physiological markers.
    
    \item \textbf{Interaction Features (3 features):} Age $\times$ APOE4, Age $\times$ cardiovascular burden, Education $\times$ current diagnosis (cognitive reserve proxy).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Target Variable Definition}

The prediction target was the \textbf{Global Clinical Dementia Rating (CDRGLOB)} at the \textit{subsequent} visit ($t+1$). We mapped CDR scores into four diagnostic classes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Class 0 (Normal):} CDR = 0
    \item \textbf{Class 1 (MCI):} CDR = 0.5 (Questionable impairment)
    \item \textbf{Class 2 (Mild Dementia):} CDR = 1.0
    \item \textbf{Class 3 (Severe Dementia):} CDR $\geq$ 2.0 (Moderate to Severe)
\end{itemize}

The target distribution exhibited class imbalance: Normal 50.8\%, MCI 26.5\%, Mild 10.8\%, Severe 11.9\%, yielding a 4.7:1 imbalance ratio.

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}

Missing values in numerical features (e.g., BMI, blood pressure) were imputed using median values stratified by current diagnosis class to preserve biological variance. Categorical variables were label-encoded. Infinite values were clipped to prevent numerical overflow.

\textbf{Patient-Level Data Splitting:} To prevent data leakage---a critical flaw where a patient's longitudinal visits appear in both training and test sets, artificially inflating accuracy---we employed \texttt{GroupShuffleSplit} stratification. All visits belonging to a specific patient ID (\texttt{NACCID}) were assigned exclusively to either the Training set (80\%, 120,965 visits from 30,106 patients) or Test set (20\%, 29,939 visits from 7,527 patients). Zero patient overlap was verified between partitions.

\subsection{Gradient Boosting Framework}

We implemented a robust predictive framework based on \textbf{XGBoost} \cite{chen2016}, selected for its superior performance on tabular clinical data and ability to handle missing values natively.

\textbf{Primary Model Configuration:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Algorithm:} XGBoost Classifier
    \item \textbf{Objective:} Multi-class Softmax
    \item \textbf{Key Hyperparameters:} 1000 estimators, learning rate 0.01, max depth 12, histogram tree method.
    \item \textbf{Regularization:} L1 ($\alpha=0.3$) and L2 ($\lambda=1.0$) regularization were applied to prevent overfitting on the high-dimensional clinical feature space.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Comparative Ensemble:}
To evaluate the necessity of complexity, we also constructed a weighted ensemble comprising XGBoost, LightGBM \cite{ke2017}, and CatBoost \cite{prokhorenkova2018}. The ensemble prediction $P_{\text{ens}}$ was computed as:
\begin{equation}
    P_{\text{ens}}(y|x) = 0.35 \cdot P_{\text{XGB}}(y|x) + 0.35 \cdot P_{\text{LGB}}(y|x) + 0.30 \cdot P_{\text{Cat}}(y|x)
\end{equation}

All models utilized early stopping (100 rounds) with 20\% validation holdout from training data.

\subsection{Class Imbalance Handling}

We applied \textbf{BorderlineSMOTE} \cite{han2005}---a variant of the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique---strictly to the training set. Unlike standard SMOTE, BorderlineSMOTE generates synthetic samples only near class decision boundaries, focusing the model on hard-to-classify transition cases (e.g., MCI patients near progression to Mild Dementia). Critically, SMOTE was applied exclusively to the training split after GroupShuffleSplit partitioning, ensuring that the test set contains only real, unaugmented patient visits with no synthetic samples. This guarantees that all reported performance metrics reflect the model's ability to generalize to genuine clinical observations. The training--test separation was verified to contain zero synthetic contamination in the evaluation partition.

Class weights were computed using inverse frequency weighting with empirical multipliers optimized for minority class performance: Normal=0.49, MCI=1.42, Mild=6.93, Severe=5.23.

\subsection{Fairness and Explainability Analysis}

To ensure equitable model performance, we evaluated accuracy and F1-macro scores stratified by:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sex:} Male vs. Female
    \item \textbf{Age Groups:} $<$65, 65--74, 75--84, 85+ years
    \item \textbf{Race:} White, Black/African American, Asian, American Indian/Alaska Native, Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander, Other/Multiracial
\end{itemize}

For interpretability, we applied SHAP \cite{lundberg2017} TreeExplainer to identify feature contributions to predictions across all diagnostic classes.

\textbf{Interpretation:} Surprisingly, the base XGBoost model without synthetic oversampling (SMOTE) or ensembling yielded the highest overall performance. Adding SMOTE marginally reduced performance (-0.8\% F1), likely due to the introduction of noise in the decision boundary overlap regions. The complex ensemble further degraded performance (-1.8\%), potentially due to conflicting decision boundaries between the component models. However, as shown in the Mild Recall column, SMOTE and ensemble diversity progressively improve Mild Dementia recall from 0.80 to 0.90 (+12\% relative), demonstrating a deliberate trade-off between overall F1 and minority-class sensitivity. These results validate a "Frugal AI" approach: a single, well-optimized model is sufficient and preferable for deployment when overall accuracy is prioritized, though ensemble configurations remain valuable when maximizing recall for clinically critical minority classes.

\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Clinical Implications}

This study demonstrates that robust dementia progression prediction is achievable using exclusively routine clinical data, without reliance on expensive neuroimaging or invasive biomarker assays. Our framework achieves 87\% accuracy and 84\% macro F1-score---performance comparable to many MRI-based models---using only demographics, cognitive assessments, medical history, and functional status indicators.

The clinical-only approach addresses the critical \textbf{accessibility gap} in current AI solutions. While MRI/PET-based models may achieve marginally higher accuracy in research settings, they cannot scale to population-level screening in primary care, community health centers, or resource-limited settings where the majority of dementia patients first present. Our framework enables:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Primary Care Deployment:} Integration with electronic health record systems for automated risk stratification during routine visits.
    \item \textbf{Resource-Limited Settings:} Applicability in low- and middle-income countries where neuroimaging infrastructure is unavailable.
    \item \textbf{Population Screening:} Cost-effective identification of high-risk individuals for targeted intervention.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Error Severity and Clinical Safety}

Beyond aggregate accuracy, our error severity analysis provides crucial insights for clinical deployment. The finding that 94.7\% of errors are 1-stage off, with only 0.2\% catastrophic errors, suggests the model maintains clinical validity even when imperfect. In practice, this means:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A Normal patient misclassified as MCI receives appropriate monitoring rather than false reassurance.
    \item A Severe patient misclassified as Mild still receives dementia care, albeit at suboptimal intensity.
    \item Catastrophic misclassifications (Normal $\leftrightarrow$ Severe) are exceedingly rare.
\end{itemize}

This error profile is far preferable to models that achieve higher accuracy by confidently classifying boundary cases, only to fail catastrophically on a small fraction of patients.

\subsection{Algorithmic Fairness}

Our fairness analysis reveals consistent performance across sex and racial groups, addressing a critical concern in healthcare AI. The model does not systematically disadvantage historically underserved populations, supporting ethical deployment in diverse clinical settings.

The modest performance decline in the 85+ age group (F1=0.813 vs. 0.856 for $<$65) reflects genuine clinical complexity rather than algorithmic bias---extremely elderly patients present with greater comorbidity burden, atypical symptom profiles, and higher diagnostic ambiguity. This finding suggests the model appropriately captures clinical reality rather than artificially homogenizing predictions across age strata.

\subsection{Feature Interpretability}

SHAP analysis identifies clinically meaningful predictors. The dominance of longitudinal diagnosis trajectory features (\texttt{diagnosis\_mean}, \texttt{worst\_ever}) confirms that disease history---the pattern of cognitive decline over time---is more prognostically informative than any single biomarker measurement. This finding aligns with clinical intuition and supports the value of longitudinal follow-up in dementia care.

The emergence of age-diagnosis interactions as strong predictors validates known epidemiological relationships: cognitive impairment in older patients carries higher progression risk than equivalent impairment in younger patients. This non-linear interaction effect would be missed by simpler linear models.

\subsection{Comparison with Neuroimaging Models}

While MRI-based deep learning models often achieve 90--95\% accuracy in binary classification tasks (e.g., AD vs. Control), direct comparison with our approach requires several caveats:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Task Complexity:} We address 4-class multi-stage classification with next-visit prediction, a more challenging task than binary classification.
    \item \textbf{Sample Heterogeneity:} The NACC cohort represents real-world clinical diversity from 40+ centers, whereas imaging studies often use homogeneous research cohorts.
    \item \textbf{Generalization:} Clinical features transfer more readily across institutions than imaging features, which are sensitive to scanner manufacturer, acquisition protocols, and site effects.
    \item \textbf{Cost-Effectiveness:} Our approach requires zero additional testing cost, making it suitable as a first-line screening tool before imaging referral.
\end{enumerate}

We propose that clinical-only and imaging-based approaches are complementary rather than competing. Our model can serve as a first-line screening tool, with imaging reserved for borderline cases or therapeutic decision-making requiring precise anatomical information.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

Several limitations merit consideration:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Dataset Bias:} The NACC cohort, while large and multi-center, is collected from specialized Alzheimer's research centers and may not fully reflect primary care populations. External validation on community-based cohorts is needed.
    
    \item \textbf{Minority Representation:} Sample sizes for some racial groups (American Indian/Alaska Native, Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander) are limited, necessitating cautious interpretation of fairness metrics for these populations.
    
    \item \textbf{Temporal Prediction Horizon:} We predict next-visit diagnosis, but the time interval between visits in the NACC cohort is variable, ranging from months to years depending on participant scheduling and retention. This variability is not explicitly modeled in the current framework; the model treats all next-visit predictions equivalently regardless of the elapsed time between assessments. Future work will adopt survival analysis approaches---such as Cox-Time or DeepSurv---to explicitly model variable time horizons and predict time-to-conversion rather than next-visit state alone.
    
    \item \textbf{Feature Availability:} Some features (e.g., APOE4 genotyping) may not be routinely available in all clinical settings. Sensitivity analysis of performance under feature missingness is warranted.
    
    \item \textbf{Synthetic Oversampling Limitations:} BorderlineSMOTE generates synthetic training samples by interpolating between existing minority-class observations, which may produce biologically implausible feature combinations that do not correspond to real patient profiles. Cost-sensitive learning with class-weighted loss functions represents a valid alternative that avoids synthetic sample generation entirely, and will be explored in future work.
\end{enumerate}

Future work will include: (1) external validation on the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) cohort, (2) survival analysis for time-to-conversion prediction, (3) integration with electronic health record systems for prospective validation, and (4) development of a clinical decision support interface for end-user deployment.

% 5. CONCLUSION
% =================================================================================
\section{Conclusion}

We presented a robust, interpretable, and fair machine learning framework for predicting dementia progression using exclusively routine clinical data. By achieving 88.6\% accuracy and 85.6\% macro F1-score on a large, multi-center cohort, this work demonstrates that expensive neuroimaging is not strictly necessary for accurate risk stratification. Notably, we showed that a single optimized XGBoost model outperforms complex ensembles, adhering to the principle of "Frugal AI".

The framework's key strengths include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical-Only Input:} No imaging, CSF, or genetic testing required beyond routine assessments.
    \item \textbf{Rigorous Validation:} Patient-level data splitting prevents information leakage and ensures honest performance estimates.
    \item \textbf{Fairness:} Consistent performance across sex, age, and racial subgroups.
    \item \textbf{Interpretability:} SHAP-based feature attribution enables clinician understanding and regulatory compliance.
    \item \textbf{Safety:} Error severity analysis confirms clinically proximal predictions even under misclassification.
\end{itemize}

This work supports a ``Frugal AI'' paradigm, democratizing access to early Alzheimer's detection in resource-constrained environments and bridging the gap between theoretical AI advances and practical clinical tools.
